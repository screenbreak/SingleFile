Why Paper Maps Still Matter

Every hiker has a story about the phone that died on the ridge. The battery gave out, or the signal vanished, or the screen was unreadable in the sun. The folded map in the side pocket, a little damp and soft at the creases, simply worked.

Paper maps do something screens rarely do: they show you everything at once. A screen shows the blue dot and a small circle of world around it. A map spread on a table shows the valley you are not walking through, the river you will cross tomorrow, and the road you could take if the weather turns.

Cartographers talk about the overview, the moment when a reader takes in the whole sheet before looking at any part of it. That overview is how people build a mental model of a place. Studies of navigation suggest that people who plan a route on paper remember it better than people who follow turn-by-turn directions.

None of this makes digital maps worse at what they do best. They are faster, they update, and they know where you are. But a map you can fold, mark with a pencil and lay flat on the kitchen table is a different kind of tool, one that asks you to look rather than follow.

The same is true of long articles. A printed essay, like a printed map, lets you see the shape of the whole before you walk through it.

Map shops report that sales of folded sheet maps have held steady for a decade, and in some regions grown. The buyers are not only walkers. Drivers who want to understand a region before a holiday, parents planning a cycle route with their children, and teachers who want a class to argue over one big sheet all still reach for paper.

There is also the pleasure of the object. A good map is a piece of design, its colours chosen to separate forest from field, its contours drawn so that a steep slope looks steep. Hanging one on a wall says something about the places you have been and the ones you mean to go.
