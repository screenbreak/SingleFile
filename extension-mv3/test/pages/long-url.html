The Library That Lends Seeds

The card catalogue in the corner of the small library looks like any other: oak drawers, brass handles, little paper labels. Pull one open and instead of index cards you find envelopes of seeds. Tomatoes in the top row, beans below, squash and pumpkins in the deep drawers at the bottom.

The rules are simple. Borrow a packet in spring, grow the plants, and bring back some seeds in the autumn. Nobody checks. The librarian keeps a notebook of what went out and what came back, and the notebook has become a record of the town's gardens.

Some varieties have done well. A yellow pear tomato that a retired teacher brought in the first year has been borrowed by more than forty households, and every autumn more of its seeds come back than went out. Others have quietly disappeared, which the librarian takes as a lesson rather than a failure: not every plant suits these hills.

Seed libraries began as a way to keep old local varieties alive. Commercial seed catalogues favour plants that travel well and ripen together. Home gardeners want something else: flavour, a long season, and plants that cope with their own soil and weather.

The library has added workshops on saving seeds, which turns out to be a skill most people have never learned. Beans are easy; you let the pods dry on the plant. Tomatoes need a few days of fermenting in a jar. Squash cross with each other so easily that a packet of saved seed can grow into something nobody has seen before.

That surprise has become part of the fun. Last year a child brought back a striped squash that no one could name, and the library gave it a drawer of its own.
