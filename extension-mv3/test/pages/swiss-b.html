The Night Train Comes Back

For most of the last thirty years the night train looked like a thing of the past. Budget airlines were cheaper, motorways were faster, and the sleeper carriages that once crossed the continent were sold, scrapped or left in sidings to rust. Timetables shrank year by year until only a few routes were left, kept alive by habit more than by demand.

Then, slowly, the passengers came back. Some wanted to fly less. Some had grown tired of airports, of the queues and the early starts and the long ride in from a terminal far outside town. And some simply remembered what it was like to fall asleep in one country and wake up in another, with the city already outside the window.

The operators noticed. New routes opened, old ones returned, and carriages that had been written off were refitted with small cabins, clean sheets and a breakfast tray at the door. The trains are often full weeks ahead, and the cheapest berths go first.

A night train is a different kind of journey. The day does not disappear into transit; it ends at the station and starts again at the other end. In between there is a dining car, a book, a view of fields going dark, and the slow rocking that sends most people to sleep sooner than they expect.

Not everything about the revival is easy. Track access is expensive, and every border means another set of rules, another crew and sometimes another locomotive. Carriages built for one network do not always fit another. The companies that run the trains talk about thin margins and long negotiations, and about routes that work on paper but not in the timetable.

Prices are a problem too. A bed on a sleeper can cost more than a seat on a plane, especially when the plane is booked early. Supporters argue that the comparison is unfair, because the train also saves a night in a hotel and starts and ends in the middle of town. Critics answer that most travellers only look at the first number they see.

Still, the trains keep filling. On a Friday evening the platforms fill with families carrying bags of food, students with rucksacks, couples on their way to a weekend away and business travellers who would rather work in a cabin than in a departure lounge. The conductor checks tickets, hands out keys and explains, again, how the folding beds work.

Some routes have become small institutions. Regulars know which carriage is quietest, which side of the train has the better view at dawn, and which station sells the best coffee during the long stop before the border. They swap these tips online and in the corridor, standing at the window while the lights of small towns pass.

The engineers who keep the old carriages running have their own stories. Parts are hard to find, so they make them. Heating systems built for one climate are adapted to another. A carriage might be fifty years old under the paint, with new wiring, new beds and the same heavy doors that close with the same sound they made when it was new.

Booking is its own small art. Some routes open their seats months ahead, others only a few weeks before the date, and a journey that crosses three countries may need three tickets from three different websites. Travel forums are full of advice on which site sells which leg, and on what to do when one train runs late and the next one leaves without you.

Inside, the cabins are small but carefully planned. A washbasin folds out of the wall, a ladder clips to the upper bunk, and every corner has a hook, a shelf or a net for something. The newest carriages add pods for single travellers, with a curtain, a reading light and a socket, at a price closer to a seat than to a bed.

Sleep is the part that worries first-time passengers most. The train stops and starts, the carriage sways, and somewhere in the night there is the clank of couplings as cars are added or taken away. Most people say they sleep better than they expected, and that the second night on a train is easier than the first.

The dining car is where the journey slows down most. Menus are short, often local to the country the train is crossing, and served on real plates. Strangers share tables because there is no other choice, and conversations start that would never start in an airport. By the time the last coffee is poured, the window shows nothing but the reflection of the car.

Railway historians point out that none of this is new. A century ago sleeping cars linked capitals across the continent, with names that still sound like adventure. What is new is the reason people give for choosing them: not luxury or romance, but the wish to travel without the carbon cost of a flight, and without giving up a whole day to do it.

That reason has changed who rides. Ten years ago the passengers were mostly older people with time to spare and a fondness for trains. Today the cabins hold young families, remote workers with laptops, and groups of friends who treat the journey as the first night of the holiday rather than as the price of getting there.

Station staff have noticed the change as well. Late evening, once the quietest part of their shift, is now busy with passengers asking where the sleeper carriages stop, whether there is time to buy food, and how early they may board. Some stations have opened their waiting rooms again after dark.

What the night train offers, in the end, is time that is not wasted. The hours are spent sleeping, reading, talking or just watching the dark, and the arrival comes with a morning already started. For a growing number of people that is worth the higher price and the narrow bed.

Whether the revival lasts depends on decisions far from the platforms: on how track access is priced, on whether governments treat cross-border trains as a public good, and on the patience of the companies that run them. For now, the tickets sell, and every evening the trains pull out on time, more or less.
