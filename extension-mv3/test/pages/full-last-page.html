The Long Afternoon of the Letter

There was a time when a letter took a week to cross a country and a month to cross an ocean, and people wrote accordingly. They wrote long, because a short letter was a waste of a stamp and of the wait. They wrote carefully, because there was no way to take a sentence back once the envelope was sealed.

The archives of almost any family hold a box of these letters. They are often dull in the way that everyday life is dull: the price of coal, a cousin's wedding, a cough that would not go away. But read enough of them and a voice comes through, and with it a sense of how slowly and how deliberately people once spoke to each other at a distance.

Historians who work with correspondence talk about the rhythm of a letter. It opens with an apology for the delay, moves through the news, circles back to answer the questions from the last letter, and closes with a list of people to be remembered to. The form was so settled that readers could skim it, yet writers still found ways to make it their own.

Email was supposed to make all of this faster and better, and in one sense it did. A message now crosses an ocean in less than a second. But the speed changed the writing. Messages became shorter, then shorter still. The greeting went, then the sign-off, then the full sentence. What was left was closer to speech than to writing, and like speech it was rarely kept.

Some people never stopped writing letters. Pen-pal clubs still match strangers across borders, and their members describe the same pleasures: choosing the paper, finding a stamp with a picture worth sending, and the small thrill of an envelope in the post that is not a bill.

Teachers have noticed that children who write letters by hand learn something that typing does not teach. A letter has to be planned. There is no delete key, so the writer thinks before the pen touches the page. Crossings-out become part of the record, and a reader can see where the writer changed their mind.

There is also the matter of keeping. A letter is an object; it can be folded into a book, tied with a ribbon, found again decades later in a drawer. Digital messages are kept too, in a sense, on servers that the writer will never see, but they are rarely read again. Few people scroll back through ten years of email for the pleasure of it.

The museums that collect letters are now worried about the gap that the last thirty years will leave. The great correspondences of the past, between scientists, between lovers, between politicians, survive because they were written on paper and kept. The equivalents of our own time are scattered across accounts that may close and formats that may not open.

Some archivists have started asking public figures to print their important messages. It is an odd request in an age of cloud storage, but paper has a record that no file format can match: it can be read without any machine at all, by anyone who knows the language, for centuries.

The craft of the envelope

Stationers who survived the decline of the letter did so by turning it into a craft. Their shops now sell heavy cotton paper, envelopes lined with patterned tissue, sealing wax in a dozen colours and fountain pens that cost more than a phone. The customers are not only older people writing to old friends. Many are in their twenties and thirties, and they describe letter writing as a kind of rest: an hour in which nothing beeps and nothing asks for a reply within the minute. Some of them met their correspondents online, on forums for people who like stamps or typewriters or a particular kind of novel, and moved the friendship onto paper because it felt more real there. A few have never met the person they write to, and say they prefer it that way; the letter is the friendship.

The stamp itself has become part of the message. Collectors choose a stamp to suit the reader, a bird for a birdwatcher, a ship for someone who lives by the sea, and post offices have learned to issue small runs of designs that people will want to send rather than keep in an album. Rubber stamps, washi tape and little drawings fill the corners of the envelope, so that the letter is already a small gift before it is opened. Postal workers in several countries report that decorated envelopes are handled with more care, and some sorting offices keep a wall of the best ones they have seen.

There are rules, too, though nobody wrote them down. You answer every letter, even if it takes a month. You never type a letter to a friend unless your handwriting has become unreadable. You keep the letters you receive, at least the good ones, and you do not show them to anyone without asking. You write about small things, because the small things are what the reader cannot get from anywhere else: the colour of the sky on the walk to work, a conversation overheard on a bus, the meal you cooked when you were too tired to cook.

What gets lost, and what does not

Linguists who study old correspondence point out that letters preserve a kind of language that is otherwise lost. Spoken language disappears as soon as it is spoken, and formal writing is edited until the voice is gone. Letters sit between the two. They keep the jokes, the spelling mistakes, the words that were fashionable for one summer and never again. A box of letters from a single family across three generations is a record of how a language changed, word by word, in the mouths of ordinary people.

Digital messages could serve the same purpose, and some researchers are trying to collect them, with permission, before they vanish. But the volume is overwhelming and the context is thin. A text message that says only that someone is on their way means nothing without the hundred messages around it, and those are rarely kept together. A letter, by contrast, was written to stand alone. It explains itself, because the writer knew it would be read days later, perhaps by someone in a different mood.

Letters in the classroom

A primary school on the coast has run a letter exchange with a school in the mountains for eleven years. Each child is paired with a child of the same age, and the pairs write once a month through the school year. The teachers do not correct the letters, though they help with addresses and stamps. At the end of the year the two classes meet for one day, halfway between the schools, and the children who have written to each other all year meet for the first time. The teachers say the first ten minutes are always silent and awkward, and then nobody can stop talking.

The children learn geography without noticing it. They learn that it snows in one place and not the other, that one class can see the sea from the playground and the other can see a glacier, that a festival in one village does not exist in the next. They also learn to wait, which is rarer. A question asked in October is answered in November, and by then the asker has often forgotten it, so the answer arrives as a small surprise. Several former pupils, now adults, still write to the partner they were given at the age of eight.

The scheme costs almost nothing. The parents' association pays for the stamps, and the paper comes from the school's own cupboard. The headteacher says it is the one project that nobody has ever asked her to measure, because everyone can see what it does. When a new teacher once suggested moving the exchange to email to save time, the children voted against it by a large margin. Their reason, written on the board in the handwriting of a nine-year-old, was simple: you cannot keep an email in a shoebox.

None of this means we should go back to waiting a week for news. But the letter offers a lesson about attention. When something takes time to write and time to arrive, it is read with care.

Postal services have their own record of the change. In the busiest years of the last century, sorting offices handled billions of personal letters, and seasonal staff were hired every December to keep up with the cards. Today the same buildings move parcels, and the personal letter is a small and shrinking share of what passes through them. Yet the people who still write say the effort is the point. A letter that took an evening to write, and three days to arrive, tells the reader that they were worth an evening and a stamp. Some writers number their letters, so that a reader knows when one has gone missing. Others keep carbon copies, or photograph each page before it goes in the envelope, building an archive of their own side of the conversation. One correspondent described the habit as keeping a diary that someone else gets to read first. The replies arrive at odd times, out of order, sometimes crossing in the post, and every one is read twice: once standing in the hall, and again later, slowly, with a cup of tea.

A long article printed and read at the kitchen table has something of that quality. It arrives, it is finished, and it waits for you.

The last letter in one family archive was written in the spring of 1987, by a grandmother to a grandson who had just moved abroad. It runs to six pages. It describes the weather, the garden, the neighbours, and a recipe for a cake he had liked as a boy. On the last page, under her signature, she added a line: write when you can, and take your time.

He did. His replies, kept in the same box, are shorter than hers and less tidy, written on paper torn from notebooks and once on the back of a train timetable. They describe a cold flat, a job he did not like at first, a friend who taught him to cook, and later a woman he would marry. The box holds both sides of a conversation that ran for nine years, until her eyesight failed and the letters became telephone calls. Nobody kept the calls. When the family cleared her house, the box of letters was the one thing that every grandchild asked to read before anything else was sorted or given away. They read it at her kitchen table, passing the pages round.
